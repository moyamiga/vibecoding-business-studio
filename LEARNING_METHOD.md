# VibeCoding Business Studio Method

## Purpose

VibeCoding Business Studio is a project-based learning method for students who
want to build real software with AI while also learning business, product
thinking, and teamwork.

The project can be a game, app, website, automation, or internal tool. The
software is the vehicle. The learning goal is to develop builders who can make
decisions, ship prototypes, work with people, and understand the business reality
around a product.

## Who It Is For

- schools;
- coding clubs;
- entrepreneurship classes;
- private learning projects;
- homeschool programs;
- youth startup programs;
- small teams learning AI-assisted software development.

## Core Roles

### Learner CEO

The learner owns day-to-day product decisions:

- vision;
- priorities;
- feature choices;
- team role assignments;
- acceptance of small milestones.

The goal is not to let AI decide everything. The goal is to help the learner make
better decisions faster.

### Mentor or Supervisor

The adult, teacher, or project supervisor owns high-risk decisions:

- money;
- publication;
- legal or privacy concerns;
- team access;
- external accounts;
- irreversible changes.

### AI Build Partner

Codex acts as:

- programmer;
- debugger;
- explainer;
- documentation assistant;
- product coach;
- project coordinator.

Codex should make progress visible, explain tradeoffs, and keep work scoped.

### Student Teammates

Students can join with clear roles:

- gameplay or product design;
- art and visual direction;
- writing and worldbuilding;
- programming;
- testing;
- marketing;
- business and monetization;
- project coordination.

Every teammate should have clear responsibilities before getting access.

## Learning Pillars

### 1. Vibe Coding

Students learn to express intent, inspect AI output, ask for changes, and build
iteratively. They do not need to start with syntax, but they must learn to verify
what was built.

### 2. Product Thinking

Every big idea is reduced into a smaller version that can be tested.

Questions:

- What is the simplest version that proves the idea?
- What must work first?
- What can wait?
- What would make a user care?

### 3. Business Reality

Technical choices are connected to business consequences.

Examples:

- If nobody knows the product exists, marketing is missing.
- If a feature costs too much to maintain, it may not be worth building.
- If a game has cosmetics, the team must avoid pay-to-win incentives.
- If teammates join, roles and expectations must be clear before conflict starts.

### 4. Teamwork

Students learn that teams need structure:

- roles;
- areas of ownership;
- approvals for cross-area changes;
- written decisions;
- clear responsibilities.

### 5. Shipping

The project must produce working milestones. A small running prototype is treated
as more valuable than a huge unfinished plan.

## Session Flow

### Step 1: Idea

The learner describes the idea in one sentence.

Output:

- product sentence;
- target user;
- why it matters.

### Step 2: Reality Check

The mentor and AI help reduce the idea to a realistic first milestone.

Output:

- first playable or usable version;
- what is included;
- what is delayed.

### Step 3: Build

Codex implements while explaining key decisions at the learner's level.

Output:

- working code;
- build/test result;
- short explanation.

### Step 4: Business Lens

The technical work is tied to one business concept.

Output:

- one business lesson;
- one question for the learner as product owner.

### Step 5: Team Coordination

If teammates are involved, role boundaries are documented.

Output:

- who owns what;
- what changed;
- who approved it.

### Step 6: Reflection

The learner records what they understood.

Output:

- concepts learned;
- questions still open;
- next small milestone.

## Assessment Rubric

### Coding

- Can the learner explain what changed?
- Can the project run?
- Were errors read and investigated?
- Is the prototype improving?

### Product

- Is the idea clear?
- Is the scope realistic?
- Can the learner explain what the user does?
- Is there a next milestone?

### Business

- Can the learner explain who the customer or player is?
- Can the learner explain cost, price, value, or marketing tradeoffs?
- Are team incentives clear?
- Are risky decisions escalated?

### Teamwork

- Does each teammate have a role?
- Are responsibilities written down?
- Are cross-area changes coordinated?
- Are conflicts handled before they become personal?

## Example: TempleFall

TempleFall is the current demo project.

Original learner ambition:

- a 3D mobile battle royale with many players, vehicles, voice, seasons, and
  cosmetics.

Reality check:

- first create a small playable prototype;
- test movement, loot, bots, objectives, and zone pressure;
- delay online multiplayer and full 3D until the core loop is fun.

Business lesson:

- a large vision must be reduced to a vertical slice before investing heavily.
  This is the same logic used by real startups and game studios.

## Safety Rules

- Do not expose private credentials or chats.
- Do not publish personal data of minors.
- Keep adult or teacher approval for public releases.
- Avoid copyrighted assets unless licensed.
- Keep monetization discussions age-appropriate and supervised.

## Repeatable Output

Each learning project should produce:

- a working prototype;
- a product description;
- a progress log;
- a learning log;
- a team-role list;
- a short demo video or presentation;
- a next-step plan.
